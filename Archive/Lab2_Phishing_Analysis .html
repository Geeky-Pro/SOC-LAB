◆ FOUNDATIONS

Introduction to Phishing

Phishing is a social engineering attack in which an attacker impersonates a trusted entity to trick a victim into revealing sensitive information, clicking a malicious link, or executing malicious code.

Most Common Vector

Phishing remains the #1 initial-access technique used in breaches, leading nearly every major attack chain.

Exploits Trust, Not Code

It targets human psychology — urgency, authority, fear, curiosity — rather than purely technical flaws.

Low Cost, High Yield

Attackers can send thousands of emails for almost no cost; even a tiny success rate yields valuable access.

Objective of a phishing attack: the ultimate goal is almost always to gain unauthorized access — to an account, a network, or funds — by manipulating a person rather than breaking a system. This lab focuses primarily on email-based phishing.

◆ ATTACK MECHANICS

How Phishing Works

Phishing works through psychological manipulation rather than technical hacking — attackers use deliberate psychological tricks and social-engineering techniques to exploit human emotion and judgment, pushing the victim to act before they stop to think.

Urgency

"Act within 24 hours" — artificial time pressure that short-circuits careful judgment.

Trust / Authority

Impersonating IT, a bank, an executive, or a known brand to borrow their credibility.

Fear

Threats of account suspension, legal trouble, or financial loss to provoke a reflexive reaction.

Familiarity

Mimicking a coworker, known vendor, or a tool the victim already uses daily, so the message feels routine rather than suspicious. Example: a fake "Slack: you have a new message" notification styled like the real thing, sent from a look-alike domain.

Scarcity

Implying a limited supply or a one-time chance, so the victim acts before thinking it through. Example: "Only 2 license renewals left at this price — confirm your Office 365 subscription now to avoid losing access."

◆ REAL-WORLD IMPACT

Real-World Case Studies

A single email, link, or stolen credential can cascade into operational shutdowns, financial ruin, and even physical-world consequences.

MAR 2011 — RSA SECURID

Spear-Phishing → Stolen Token Seeds

A spear-phishing email titled "2011 Recruitment Plan" carried an Excel attachment with a hidden Adobe Flash zero-day, letting attackers exfiltrate SecurID seed values later used against defense contractors.

$66.3M remediation · 30K+ customers affected
SEP 2020 — LEVITAS CAPITAL

Fake Zoom Invite

A co-founder clicked a fake Zoom invite link, installing malware that gave attackers control of the firm's email and let them approve fraudulent wire transfers.

$781K lost · fund closed
DEC 2015 — UKRAINE'S POWER GRID

Spear-Phishing → BlackEnergy

Spear-phishing emails with malicious Office macro attachments delivered BlackEnergy malware seven months before Sandworm cut the power.

230,000 customers · 1–6 hrs outage
JUN 2015 — UBIQUITI NETWORKS

CEO Fraud / BEC

Attackers impersonated executives by email to trick the finance department into wiring funds overseas — no system breach required.

$46.7M stolen · $8.1M recovered

Analyst's note: Even a security vendor specializing in authentication was breached by a deliberately unremarkable phishing email — proof that technical sophistication elsewhere doesn't compensate for one person opening one attachment.

◆ TAXONOMY

Phishing Attack Types

Phishing isn't one technique — it's a category with several variants, differing by delivery channel and how precisely the target is selected.

Phishing

Mass, generic emails cast wide to as many recipients as possible, hoping a small percentage bite.

Spear-Phishing

Targeted at a specific individual or organization, personalized with researched details to seem credible.

Whaling

Spear-phishing aimed specifically at "big fish" — executives, finance officers, or other high-value targets.

Smishing

Phishing delivered over SMS text messages, often with a malicious link disguised as a delivery or bank alert.

Vishing

Voice-call phishing — attackers impersonate IT support, banks, or government over the phone to extract info.

Quishing

QR-code phishing — a malicious QR code redirects a scanning phone to a credential-harvesting page.

◆ ATTACKER MOTIVATION

Common Attacker Objectives

Regardless of the channel used, most phishing campaigns are working toward one of three underlying goals.

Information Gathering

Reconnaissance to map an organization's structure, vendors, or systems — fuel for a later, more convincing attack. Attackers favor this approach because it lets them collect intelligence without direct interaction whenever possible, staying unnoticed while they build a profile of the target.

Credential Harvesting

Tricking the victim into typing their username and password into a fake login page the attacker controls.

Malware Delivery

Using a link or attachment to get malicious code running directly on the victim's device.

Note: These objectives often chain together — information gathering in one campaign can feed a more convincing spear-phish later, which then harvests credentials or delivers malware.

◆ ATTACK TECHNIQUES

Phishing Attack Techniques

Beyond the message itself, attackers rely on specific technical tricks to make the deception convincing and to slip past filters.

URL Manipulation

Look-alike domains, typosquatting, URL shorteners, and subdomain tricks that disguise a malicious link's real destination.

  • Typosquatting: micros0ft.com or paypa1.com — a slight misspelling close enough to pass a quick glance.
  • Homograph attack: аpple.com using a Cyrillic "а" instead of Latin "a" — visually identical, entirely different domain.
  • URL shortener: bit.ly/3xK9z2 hides the real destination until after the click.

Email Spoofing & Impersonation

Forging the From: address or display name so the message appears to come from a trusted sender, exploiting the lack of built-in sender verification in SMTP.

Encoding

Obfuscating links or payloads with Base64, URL encoding, or HTML character entities so the malicious content is unreadable to filters but renders normally for the victim.

Malicious Attachments

Weaponized files — macro-enabled documents, double extensions, or password-protected archives — that deliver malware once the victim opens them.

Abuse of Legitimate Services

Hosting phishing pages on trusted platforms (Google Forms, SharePoint, cloud storage) so links pass reputation and spam filters undetected.

◆ ANATOMY OF AN EMAIL

Email Structure

Every email — legitimate or malicious — is built from the same basic parts. Knowing what's normal in each makes anomalies easier to spot.

To: j.doe@company.com   Subject: Action Required: Verify Your Account
BODY

↑ Header

Routing metadata: From, To, Subject, Received hops, and authentication results.

↑ Body

The visible message content — text, HTML formatting, and embedded social-engineering cues.

↑ URLs

Embedded links — often disguised, shortened, or pointing to look-alike domains.

↑ Attachments

Files riding along with the message — common vehicle for malware delivery.

◆ COMPONENT 01

Email Header

The header carries routing and authentication metadata — usually hidden from the average user, but essential for an analyst.

HEADER
From: "IT Support" <support@accounts-secure-verify.com>
To: j.doe@company.com
Subject: Action Required: Verify Your Account
Received: from mail.accounts-secure-verify.com [203.0.113.42] ...
SPF: fail   DKIM: none   DMARC: fail

From / Display Name

Easily spoofed — attackers set any display name they like, regardless of the actual sending address.

Received Chain

Each mail hop adds a line — read bottom-up to trace the message's true origin path.

SPF / DKIM / DMARC

Authentication results that verify whether the sending server was actually authorized for that domain.

◆ COMPONENT 02

Email Body

The body is where social engineering happens — the visible text and formatting designed to provoke a reaction.

BODY
Dear Customer,

We detected unusual activity on your account. Your access will be suspended within 24 hours unless you verify your identity immediately.

Verify My Account →

Common Manipulation Tactics

  • Urgency — artificial deadlines and threats of account loss
  • Authority — impersonating IT, executives, or known brands
  • Fear — warnings of security breaches or legal action

What to Examine

  • Generic greetings ("Dear Customer") instead of your actual name
  • Mismatched branding, logos, or formatting quality
  • Hidden link destinations beneath styled button text
◆ COMPONENT 03

URLs in Phishing Emails

Malicious links rarely look obviously malicious — attackers rely on visual tricks to disguise the real destination.

URL
Displayed text: www.paypal.com/account/verify
Actual destination: hxxp://paypa1-secure.verify-account.ru/login.php

Look-alike Domains

Character substitution (paypa1.com), extra words (paypal-secure.com), or different TLDs (.ru, .cn, .xyz).

URL Shorteners

bit.ly, tinyurl, and similar services hide the real destination behind a short, unreadable link.

Subdomain Abuse

paypal.com.verify-login.net — the real domain is "verify-login.net"; "paypal.com" is just a subdomain label.

Analyst's tip: Always hover over (never click) a link to preview the real URL, and read domains from right to left, identifying the actual registrable domain before trusting anything in front of it.

◆ COMPONENT 04

Malicious Attachments

Attachments remain one of the most direct ways to deliver malware — once opened, the victim's own actions execute the payload.

ATTACHMENT
Invoice_2024.pdf.exe (1.2 MB)

Double Extensions

Invoice.pdf.exe — Windows hides the real extension by default, so it appears as a harmless PDF.

Macro-Enabled Office Files

.docm / .xlsm files that prompt "Enable Content" to run a malicious macro on open — used in the Ukraine grid attack.

Password-Protected Archives

ZIP files with the password inside the email body, specifically to evade automated antivirus/sandbox scanning.

ISO / IMG Containers

Disk image files that bypass "Mark of the Web" browser-download warnings applied to regular files.

Email Fundamentals

To analyze a phishing email effectively, you first need to understand how legitimate email actually travels across the internet — the client, the mail servers, the DNS lookup, and the agents and protocols involved.

Why this matters: Spoofed headers, mismatched routing, and forged sender fields are easiest to catch when you know what authentic email transmission looks like under the hood.

MUA

Mail User Agent

The email client the person actually uses — Outlook, Gmail's web interface, Apple Mail. It composes, sends, and displays messages on behalf of the user.

MTA

Mail Transfer Agent

The server software that relays mail between servers using SMTP — it's what moves a message from Bob's provider, across the internet, to Alice's provider.

MDA

Mail Delivery Agent

Takes the message handed off by the final MTA and deposits it into the recipient's actual mailbox, where it waits to be retrieved via POP3/IMAP.

Analyst's tip: Gmail and Yahoo each run both the MTA (relay) and MDA (mailbox delivery) roles on the same servers — they're logically distinct agents, but commonly bundled into one platform. Each MTA hop in between adds a Received: line you can trace in the header.

◆ PROTOCOLS

Email Protocols

A small set of protocols, each with one job, work together to move and retrieve every email.

SMTP (port 25 / 587)

Simple Mail Transfer Protocol — used to send and relay mail between the client and outgoing servers, and between servers.

DNS (MX Record) (port 53)

Resolves which mail server is responsible for a domain — checked before delivery so the message reaches the right destination.

POP3 (port 110)

Post Office Protocol — downloads mail to one device and typically removes it from the server.

IMAP (port 143)

Internet Message Access Protocol — keeps mail synced on the server, so multiple devices see the same mailbox state.

Analyst's tip: None of these protocols verify sender identity by default — that's why SPF, DKIM, and DMARC exist as separate authentication layers checked at the header level.

◆ METHOD DEEP DIVE

Header Analysis

The header is the first place to verify whether a message is what it claims to be — most of it is invisible to the average recipient.

Trace the Received: Chain

Each mail hop prepends a Received: line. Read from the bottom up — the bottom-most entry is closest to the true origin, since every later hop is added on top.

Compare From / Reply-To / Return-Path

Legitimate mail usually has these aligned to the same domain. A mismatch — e.g. From: your bank, Reply-To: a free webmail address — is a strong red flag.

Check the Sending IP

Cross-reference the originating IP in the first Received: line against the domain's known mail infrastructure or a reputation/geolocation lookup.

Inspect the Display Name

"IT Support" can sit in front of any address. Always check the actual address in angle brackets, not just the friendly name shown by the mail client.

Analyst's tip: Header fields like From: are trivially spoofed because SMTP has no built-in sender verification — that's exactly why authentication protocols like SPF, DKIM, and DMARC exist as a separate check, covered next.

◆ METHOD DEEP DIVE

Authentication Analysis

SMTP never verified who a sender really was — SPF, DKIM, and DMARC were added afterward to close that gap. An analyst checks all three results in the header.

SPF

Sender Policy Framework — the domain publishes a DNS record listing which IPs are allowed to send mail for it. The receiving server checks the sending IP against that list.

Result: pass / fail / softfail / none

DKIM

DomainKeys Identified Mail — the sender signs the message with a private key; the receiver checks that signature against a public key published in DNS to confirm the message wasn't altered in transit.

Result: pass / fail / none

DMARC

Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance — ties SPF and DKIM together and tells receiving servers what to do if both fail: monitor, quarantine, or reject.

Result: pass / fail (policy: none / quarantine / reject)

HEADER
From: "IT Support" <support@accounts-secure-verify.com>
SPF: fail   DKIM: none   DMARC: fail (p=reject)

Analyst's tip: All three failing at once, as above, is a near-certain spoof. But check individually — a forwarded legitimate email can fail SPF/DKIM for benign reasons, so always read the result in context rather than treating any single failure as definitive proof.

◆ METHOD DEEP DIVE

Content Analysis

With the header and authentication checked, the next step is reading the body itself closely for signs it wasn't written — or wasn't meant to be read — the normal way.

Grammar & Spelling Errors

Awkward phrasing, inconsistent tense, and spelling mistakes are common in phishing emails, especially those translated or template-generated rather than written natively by the claimed sender.

Urgency & Pressure Language

Phrases like "act now," "your account will be suspended," or "verify immediately" are designed to short-circuit careful reading — a legitimate sender rarely needs a countdown.

Encoded or Hidden Content

Check the raw source for Base64-encoded blocks, suspicious HTML entities, or zero-width/invisible characters used to hide text from spam filters while still rendering to the reader.

Formatting & Branding Inconsistencies

Mismatched logos, off-brand colors or fonts, and a signature that doesn't match the claimed organization's usual style are easy to miss at a glance but stand out under close reading.

Analyst's tip: Always view the raw source rather than the rendered email — hidden characters and encoded payloads are invisible in a normal mail client but stand out immediately in plain text.
