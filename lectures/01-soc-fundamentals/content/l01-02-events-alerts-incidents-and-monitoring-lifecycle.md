# L01-02 — Security Events, Alerts, Incidents, and the Monitoring Lifecycle

> **Status:** draft  
> **Module:** 01-soc-fundamentals  
> **Duration:** 45 minutes  
> **Outcomes:** LO-01-02  
> **Audience:** Entry-level SOC learners

## Learning outcomes

Learners will be able to distinguish an event, alert, indicator, case, incident, and finding, and explain how observations move through a simplified monitoring workflow.

## 1. Start with observations, not conclusions

A SOC receives records from many sources: operating systems, identity systems, network devices, applications, cloud services, email systems, and security products. A record is evidence of something observed or reported. It does not automatically explain intent or prove malicious activity.

Consider these statements:
- “The identity system recorded 12 failed sign-in attempts.”
- “The monitoring rule generated an alert for repeated failures.”
- “The analyst opened a case to investigate the activity.”
- “The investigation found that a user mistyped a password while travelling.”
- “The organization confirmed an incident after additional evidence showed account compromise.”

Each statement represents a different stage or type of information.

## 2. Core terms

| Term | Practical meaning | Example | What it does not prove by itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Event | A recorded occurrence or state change in a system or service. | A successful sign-in record. | Whether the action was malicious or unauthorized. |
| Telemetry | Data collected about system, network, identity, or application activity. | Process creation records or firewall logs. | That the data is complete or correctly interpreted. |
| Indicator | An observable value or pattern that may help identify activity. | A domain, file hash, IP address, or behavior pattern. | That the value is malicious in every context. |
| Alert | A notification generated by a rule, analytic, product, or person for review. | Repeated failed logins trigger a rule. | That a security incident occurred. |
| Case | A managed record used to track an alert or investigation, evidence, actions, ownership, and disposition. | Case SOC-2026-001. | That the case is necessarily a confirmed incident. |
| Incident | An event or group of events assessed under organizational policy as a cybersecurity incident requiring coordinated response. | Confirmed unauthorized access to an account and data. | That every detail or impact is already known. |
| Finding | A conclusion supported by the investigation evidence. | “The sign-in originated from the expected corporate VPN.” | That the finding cannot change if new evidence appears. |

Organizations may define these terms differently. In this course, use the working definitions above and always follow the policy of the organization being supported.

## 3. A simplified monitoring-to-case lifecycle

1. **Collect:** a source produces telemetry.
2. **Normalize and enrich:** systems may parse fields and add context such as asset or identity information.
3. **Detect:** a rule or analytic identifies a pattern of interest.
4. **Alert:** a notification is created for review.
5. **Triage:** an analyst checks validity, scope, context, and urgency.
6. **Investigate:** the analyst gathers and correlates evidence.
7. **Disposition or escalate:** the case is closed with a supported reason, kept open for more work, or escalated under policy.
8. **Learn:** recurring issues, gaps, and lessons inform improvements.

This is a teaching model, not a required product architecture. Real workflows may combine, reorder, or automate steps.

## 4. Alert is not the same as incident

A detection rule is designed to identify a pattern. It may generate:
- A true positive: the alert correctly identifies activity of concern.
- A false positive: the alert fires on activity that does not meet the intended detection condition.
- A benign true positive: the behavior occurred and matched the rule, but is authorized or expected in context.
- A false negative: relevant activity occurred but the detection did not alert.

These terms require care. “True positive” describes detection behavior against a defined condition; it does not automatically mean a confirmed security incident. “False positive” should not be used as a shortcut when the analyst has not investigated sufficiently.

## 5. Example: repeated authentication failures

An identity source records multiple failed sign-ins for one account. A rule creates an alert.

Possible explanations include:
- A user repeatedly entered an incorrect password.
- A stale service or application is using an old password.
- A legitimate user is connecting from an unfamiliar location.
- Someone is attempting password guessing.
- A compromised device is generating automated attempts.

The event and alert are real observations. The cause is a hypothesis. Additional context is needed before deciding whether this is benign, suspicious, or an incident.

Useful context may include:
- Whether a successful sign-in followed the failures.
- Whether the account is privileged or service-related.
- Whether the source device and network are expected.
- Whether other accounts show similar activity.
- Whether the user or system owner can validate the behavior.
- Whether the time window aligns with normal activity.

## 6. What a good analyst says

Weak statement:
> “The account was hacked.”

Evidence-based statement:
> “The identity source recorded 12 failed sign-ins for account A from source address B between 09:02 and 09:06 UTC. A successful sign-in followed at 09:07 UTC. The source and device context have not yet been validated. Account compromise is a hypothesis, not a confirmed finding.”

The second statement separates facts from interpretation and names what remains unknown.

## 7. Classification exercise

For each item, choose the best label: event, indicator, alert, case, incident, or finding. Some items may involve more than one concept; explain your reasoning.

1. A Windows log records a new process starting.
2. A domain name is included in a threat-intelligence report.
3. A rule creates a notification because a process launched from an unusual location.
4. An analyst opens a tracked investigation record and assigns an owner.
5. A company confirms unauthorized access and begins its incident-response process.
6. After validating approved maintenance, the analyst concludes the unusual process was expected.

**Instructor answer guide:**
1. Event.
2. Indicator (with source and confidence context required).
3. Alert.
4. Case.
5. Incident (subject to the organization's incident definition).
6. Finding/disposition; retain the evidence and rationale.

## 8. Knowledge check

1. Can an alert exist without a confirmed incident? Explain.
2. Why should an indicator be assessed in context?
3. What is the difference between a case and an incident?
4. Why is “false positive” an unsafe conclusion before adequate triage?

## 9. Summary

- Events and telemetry are observations; alerts are notifications; cases track work; incidents are determined under organizational policy.
- Indicators are clues, not verdicts.
- Analysts should distinguish detection accuracy from incident confirmation.
- Every conclusion should be traceable to evidence and state its limitations.
